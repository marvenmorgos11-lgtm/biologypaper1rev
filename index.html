⚡ Exam Tip Bacteria are prokaryotes — they have NO nucleus. Their DNA floats freely in the cytoplasm.
4.1.1.2 Animal & Plant Cells

Both have

Nucleus — contains DNA, controls cell activity
Cytoplasm — jelly-like; where reactions occur
Cell membrane — controls what enters/exits
Mitochondria — site of aerobic respiration (energy release)
Ribosomes — where protein synthesis occurs

Plant cells also have

Cell wall — cellulose; gives strength/support
Chloroplasts — contain chlorophyll; absorb light for photosynthesis
Permanent vacuole — filled with cell sap; keeps cell turgid
⚡ Exam Tip Learn the formula: Magnification = Size of image ÷ Real size of object. Electron microscopes have higher magnification AND better resolution than light microscopes.

4.1.2.2 Mitosis & The Cell Cycle
Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells — used for growth, repair and replacement.
Steps of Mitosis
1. Growth: Cell grows; DNA replicates → chromosomes are now double-stranded.
2. Condensation: Chromosomes condense and become visible.
3. Alignment: Nuclear membrane breaks down; chromosomes line up at the centre.
4. Separation: One copy of each chromosome pulled to each end of the cell.
5. Division: Cytoplasm divides → two identical cells formed.
⚡ Exam Tip Humans have 23 pairs = 46 chromosomes. After mitosis, each daughter cell has the same number (46). Don't confuse with meiosis (not in B1–4).
4.1.2.3 Stem Cells
Stem cells are undifferentiated cells that can divide and develop into specialised cells.

Human stem cells

Embryonic: can become almost any cell type (totipotent); used in research
Adult (bone marrow): can form blood cells; used in leukaemia treatment

Plant meristems

Found at root and shoot tips
Can differentiate into any plant cell type throughout life
Used to clone plants cheaply
Medical UsesPotential to treat: diabetes (grow new insulin-producing cells), paralysis (grow nerve cells). Ethical debate around embryonic stem cell use.
4.1.3.1–3 Diffusion, Osmosis & Active Transport

DiffusionNet movement of particles from high → low concentrationNoDown conc. gradient

⚡ Exam Tip — Osmosis In a concentrated solution, cells lose water → shrivel/plasmolyse. In a dilute solution, cells gain water → become turgid. Remember: water moves to where there is MORE solute.
🔬 Required Practical 2 — Osmosis in potato Cut equal cylinders of potato → place in solutions of different salt/sugar concentrations → measure % change in mass. Higher concentration = potato loses mass (water moves out by osmosis).
Active transport example: Root hair cells absorb nitrate ions from the soil (low concentration → high concentration) using energy from respiration.

Infection & Response

4.3.1 Communicable Disease · Immunity · Drugs

4.3.1.1 Communicable Diseases & Pathogens
A pathogen is a microorganism that causes infectious disease. Types: viruses, bacteria, fungi, protists.
Bacteria reproduce rapidly inside body; produce toxins that damage tissues.
Viruses live INSIDE cells, reproduce inside cells → cause cell damage.
Spread by: direct contact, water, air (droplets), vectors (e.g. mosquitoes).

4.3.1.6 Human Defence Systems
1st Line of Defence (Non-specific)
Skin — physical barrier; prevents pathogen entry
Nose hairs & mucus — trap pathogens before they reach lungs
Cilia in trachea — waft mucus with trapped pathogens up to throat to be swallowed
Stomach acid (HCl) — kills pathogens in food and swallowed mucus
2nd Line of Defence (Immune System)

Phagocytes

Engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis
Non-specific — attack all pathogens

Lymphocytes

Detect antigens on pathogens → produce antibodies (specific to antigen)
Also produce antitoxins to neutralise toxins
Create memory cells for faster future response
4.3.1.7 Vaccination
A vaccine introduces dead or inactive forms of a pathogen → stimulates white blood cells to produce antibodies and memory cells.
If the same pathogen enters again, the immune system responds faster and more strongly (secondary immune response).
Herd ImmunityIf enough of a population is vaccinated, the disease cannot spread easily — protecting those who cannot be vaccinated (e.g. newborns, immunocompromised). This requires a very high uptake rate.
⚡ Exam Tip Vaccines do NOT give you the disease. Memory cells mean future response is FASTER and LARGER than first exposure.
4.3.1.8–9 Antibiotics, Painkillers & Drug Development
Antibiotics (e.g. penicillin) kill bacteria inside the body but cannot kill viruses.
Painkillers treat symptoms but do NOT kill pathogens.
Antibiotic resistance is growing — caused by: overuse, not completing full course, use in farming. MRSA is a well-known superbug.
Drug Development Process
1. Discovery: Traditionally from plants/microorganisms (aspirin from willow; penicillin from Penicillium mould; digitalis from foxgloves). Now also synthesised in labs.
2. Preclinical testing: Tested on cells, tissues and live animals for toxicity, efficacy and dose.
3. Clinical trials: Tested on healthy volunteers (safety) then patients (effectiveness). Very low doses first; dose increased if safe.
4. Double-blind trial: Some patients get a placebo; neither patients nor doctors know who gets what — removes bias.

Bioenergetics

4.4.1 Photosynthesis · 4.4.2 Respiration & Metabolism

4.4.1.1 The Photosynthetic Reaction
carbon dioxide + water →light glucose + oxygen
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
Photosynthesis is an endothermic reaction — energy is transferred from the environment to the chloroplasts by light.
Takes place in chloroplasts (contain chlorophyll to absorb light energy).
4.4.1.2 Rate of Photosynthesis — Limiting Factors
FactorEffect on rateWhy
Light intensity ↑Rate increases (then plateaus)More energy for reaction; plateaus when another factor limits
CO₂ concentration ↑Rate increases (then plateaus)More raw material available; plateaus when light/temp limits
Temperature ↑Rate increases then drops sharplyEnzymes work faster → then denature above ~45°C
Chlorophyll amount ↑Rate increasesMore pigment to absorb light
⚡ Exam Tip — Limiting Factor When the rate plateaus despite increasing one factor, a DIFFERENT factor is now limiting. E.g. adding more light eventually stops increasing rate if CO₂ runs out.
🔬 Required Practical 5 — Rate of Photosynthesis (pondweed) Place pondweed (e.g. Elodea) in water at different distances from a lamp. Count oxygen bubbles produced per minute (= rate of photosynthesis). Move lamp further away → less light → fewer bubbles. Use a light meter for accuracy.
4.4.1.3 Uses of Glucose from Photosynthesis
Respiration — releases energy for the plant's life processes
Starch — insoluble storage form of carbohydrate (in cells and storage organs)
Cellulose — used to build and strengthen cell walls
Lipids — stored in seeds for energy
Proteins — glucose + nitrates from soil → amino acids → proteins (for growth and repair)
4.4.2.1 Aerobic & Anaerobic Respiration
Aerobic Respiration
glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy released)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy
Occurs in mitochondria. Releases a large amount of energy. Continuous process in all living cells.
Anaerobic Respiration
Occurs when oxygen is in short supply. Releases less energy than aerobic. Occurs in cytoplasm.
OrganismEquationProducts
Human muscleglucose → lactic acid + energyLactic acid (causes muscle fatigue/pain)
Yeast / some plantsglucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energyEthanol + CO₂ (used in brewing/bread-making)
AerobicAnaerobic
O₂ needed?YesNo

⚡ Exam Tip Respiration is NOT the same as breathing. Respiration is a chemical reaction inside CELLS. Breathing (ventilation) moves air in/out of lungs.
4.4.2.2 Response to Exercise
During exercise, muscles need more energy → heart rate ↑, breathing rate ↑, tidal volume ↑ to deliver more O₂ and remove CO₂.
If O₂ supply cannot keep up → muscles respire anaerobically → lactic acid builds up → muscle fatigue.
Oxygen debt: Extra oxygen needed after exercise to break down lactic acid. Lactic acid is transported to the liver in the blood → oxidised to CO₂ + H₂O, or converted back to glucose/glycogen. This is why you keep breathing fast after exercise.
GlycogenGlucose is stored as glycogen in muscles and liver. During long exercise, glycogen reserves deplete → glucose transported from liver → eventually leads to muscle fatigue.
4.4.2.3 Metabolism
Metabolism = the sum of ALL chemical reactions occurring in an organism's cells, controlled by enzymes.

Building up (anabolism)

Glucose → starch/glycogen (storage)
Glucose + nitrates → amino acids → proteins
Glucose → cellulose (cell walls)
Fatty acids + glycerol → lipids (cell membranes/seed storage)

Breaking down (catabolism)

Glucose → CO₂ + H₂O (respiration)
Excess amino acids → urea (in liver) → excreted by kidneys
Lipids → fatty acids + glycerol (energy)
Glycogen → glucose (when energy needed)
